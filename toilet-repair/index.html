Toilet Repair in Leander, TX

Toilet repair for running tanks, weak flushes, leaks, and faulty fill valves.

Call (512) 698-2730 Ask a question

A toilet that keeps refilling after every flush can go from an irritating sound in the bathroom to water running through the tank day and night. A flapper that no longer seals may let water slip into the bowl, while a fill valve that sticks can keep the tank from reaching the right water level. Over a week or a month, mineral buildup, worn rubber, and a constantly moving valve can turn a small tank fault into more parts needing attention.

Water around the toilet base is different from a noisy tank. A loose supply connection can drip behind the bowl, and a failing wax ring can allow water to reach the flooring around the toilet flange. Moisture under vinyl, tile, or a bathroom cabinet can damage the subfloor if it is left in place through a season.

A loose toilet seat or a handle that occasionally needs a second push can often be scheduled, but a toilet that runs continuously, overflows, leaks at the base, or will not flush should not wait. Those faults involve water moving where it should not be—through the bowl, out of the tank, or beneath the toilet—and the bathroom floor can get worse while the fixture remains in use.

Toilet keeps running

We hear several different descriptions when a toilet has stopped working properly: “the toilet keeps running,” “the tank will not fill,” “the handle is loose,” and “the toilet flushes but the bowl stays full.” A hiss from the tank after flushing can point to a fill valve or refill tube, while water trickling into the bowl often points to a flapper, flush valve seat, or tank gasket. A toilet that needs the handle held down may have a chain that is too long, too short, tangled, or disconnected from the lever.

A weak flush, bubbling water in the bowl, a toilet that rocks on the floor, and water appearing near the base each lead us to different components. The symptoms can sound alike from the bathroom doorway, but a tank seal, fill valve, closet bolt, wax ring, and bowl passage are separate faults with different parts and labor behind them. We inspect the fixture before treating one symptom as another.

Tank and bowl inspection

During an inspection, we remove the tank lid, flush the toilet, and watch the flapper, chain, overflow tube, fill valve, and refill tube through a complete cycle. We check the tank water level, look for a slow drop after the valve shuts off, test the handle movement, and inspect the supply line and angle stop for moisture. Where a pressure concern is present, we can check the water supply with a gauge; residential plumbing pressure is commonly kept between 40 and 80 psi.

Basic tank checks commonly take 15 to 30 minutes, and a standard flapper, handle, fill valve, or tank-to-bowl gasket replacement can often be completed during the same visit when the correct compatible part is available. We may need to book follow-up work for a cracked bowl, a seized shutoff valve, a toilet that must be removed for flange access, or a model-specific part that has to be ordered. If we lift the toilet, we inspect the flange area, bolts, wax ring, and condition of the flooring before reinstalling the fixture.

Shutoff valves and tank parts

The condition of the tank hardware affects the scope of a toilet repair. A worn flapper or fill valve in an accessible tank is a different job from a corroded flush valve seat, damaged tank bolts, or a cracked porcelain tank. Older toilets may use discontinued handles, unusual flush valves, or proprietary parts that require a matching replacement rather than a universal component.

Access at the water supply also changes the work involved. A working angle stop lets us isolate the toilet at the wall, while a seized shutoff valve can require attention to the supply connection before tank parts can be changed. A toilet set tightly between a vanity and wall, a fixture with damaged floor bolts, or a part that must be ordered adds labor or another visit; clear access to the tank and shutoff keeps the repair more straightforward.

Tank model and bathroom

When you call, tell us which bathroom has the toilet and whether the tank is running, leaking, overflowing, or failing to refill. A photo of the tank interior, flush handle, and water supply connection can help us identify the fill valve, flapper style, and flush valve arrangement. If you can find a make or model marking, it is often printed inside the tank near the water line or on the underside of the lid. Let us know the approximate age of the toilet and whether a vanity, cabinet, or tight wall space limits access to the shutoff valve.
