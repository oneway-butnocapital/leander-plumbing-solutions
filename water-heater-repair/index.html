Water Heater Repair in Leander, TX

Water heater diagnosis and repair for leaking, cold, noisy, and unreliable units.

Call (512) 698-2730 Ask a question

A dry utility closet paired with cold water at the shower or kitchen faucet is one kind of water-heater problem. A wet patch below the tank, water at the drain pan, or a steady drip from the relief valve is another. Both can be called a broken water heater from the hallway, but one starts with heat production while the other starts with the tank, pipe connection, valve, or drain line.

We handle both heating faults and water around the unit. On electric models, the problem may sit at an element, thermostat, wiring terminal, or reset button. On gas models, we check the pilot, igniter, burner, vent connection, gas control, and combustion area. A leak can come from a fitting or valve that can be repaired, while water coming through a corroded tank shell points to a different decision.

A dry loss of hot water can sometimes wait for a planned repair when the tank and connections are dry. Water beneath the heater, discharge from the temperature-and-pressure relief valve, a gas smell, or scorching near a wire connection should not wait, because a water-heater fault can damage the floor, affect the gas or electrical supply, or allow pressure and heat problems to continue.

No hot water or leaks

We hear “no hot water,” “the shower goes cold fast,” “the water heater is making a popping sound,” and “there is water under the tank.” Those descriptions matter because each one leads us toward different parts. Cold water at every fixture can involve an electric element, upper thermostat, gas burner, pilot assembly, or control valve, while a shower that starts hot and turns cold may also involve the tank’s stored-water capacity or a worn dip tube.

A popping or rumbling tank often points to mineral sediment on the bottom of the vessel. Water from the relief-valve pipe may involve excess water pressure, thermal expansion, or the valve itself; water at the top of the unit can come from the cold inlet, hot outlet, dielectric fitting, or pipe joint. Rust-colored water, moisture around the seam, and water collecting from below the jacket can indicate internal tank corrosion rather than an external fitting.

These faults can look alike without removing an access panel, checking the fittings, and testing the controls, but they use different parts and require different repair work. We identify the source before we recommend a repair path.

Tank inspection

At the inspection, we start with the tank label, fuel or electrical supply, visible pipe connections, shutoff valve, drain valve, relief-valve discharge pipe, and the area below the unit. We check whether the water is leaking from a threaded fitting, a valve body, the drain pan, or the tank itself. For an electric heater, we test voltage at the disconnect and terminals, then check the heating elements and thermostats after isolating power. For a gas heater, we inspect the burner compartment, pilot or ignition sequence, venting, combustion air, and control settings.

We can measure incoming water pressure, which is commonly expected to stay within a range of about 40 to 80 psi in a residential plumbing system, and compare the temperature setting with water at a nearby faucet. A basic diagnosis commonly takes about 30 to 60 minutes, depending on access, the heater location, and whether panels, piping, or a closet door need to be moved. Draining sediment or replacing a part takes longer because the tank must cool, isolate, and refill before we can confirm the result.

When the correct element, thermostat, relief valve, drain valve, igniter, or connection fitting is available and the tank is sound, we can often complete that repair during the same visit. A corroded tank, a specialty gas control, a unit with restricted venting access, or a part that must be ordered is planned for a return visit, along with any required installation or sign-off work.

Shutoff valves and access

The age and condition of the failed part affect the repair scope. A replaceable thermostat, element, relief valve, or pipe fitting is different from a tank shell that has rusted through at the seam. Older units can also have brittle wiring insulation, corroded nipples, seized drain valves, or fittings that do not separate cleanly without replacing adjoining pipe.

Access around the tank changes the work involved. A heater in an open garage offers a different working area from one in a tight utility closet, attic platform, or cabinet with a water-softener line beside it. We need room to isolate the water, remove access panels, drain the vessel where needed, and safely reach the electrical disconnect, gas shutoff, or vent connection.

A shutoff valve that will not close can add pipe and valve work before a component can be changed. A model-specific gas control, burner assembly, or electronic part may need to be ordered, and a replacement tank can involve venting, drain-pan, discharge-pipe, electrical, gas, or local sign-off requirements. Those conditions determine the labor and materials involved more accurately than the symptom alone.

Model plate and tank age

Before calling, take a photo of the model and serial-number plate, usually mounted on the side of the tank or behind an outer panel. Let us know whether the unit is gas or electric, which room or closet holds it, and whether water is currently collecting beneath the tank, at the pipe connections, or from the relief-valve discharge pipe. The tank age, if you know it, and a photo of the burner compartment or electrical access panel can help us identify likely parts. Tell us whether the water is completely cold, runs out quickly, smells unusual, or is making a popping sound, and whether a gate, door, attic opening, or cabinet limits access.
